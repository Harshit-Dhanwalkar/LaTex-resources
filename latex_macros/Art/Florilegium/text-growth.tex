\subsubsection{The growth stage}

This document is the answer to the exercise. Its margin is not the
authored composition of
the previous section: nothing in it was placed by hand. A Lua program
decides where every
stem, leaf and flower goes and how large it is, and then calls the
same primitives to paint
them.

\begin{construction}[Stems]
  The root is placed near the bottom-left corner of the page. Three
  stems rise from it; the
  $x$ coordinate of each is a smooth function of the height,
  \[
    x(y)=c+A\,n\bigl(y/\lambda+\varphi\bigr),
    \qquad A=0.2\,\text{(column width)},\ \lambda\in[6,9]\,\text{cm},
  \]
  with $n$ a two-octave gradient noise and $\varphi$ a phase drawn
  per stem, blended into the
  root over the first four centimeters. Different phases make the
  stems sway apart and cross
  now and then, as real stems do. The first reaches the top of the
  page and ends in the
  largest flower; the second stops at $60$ to $72\%$ of the height
  and ends in a lily; the
  third at $36$ to $48\%$, in a flower. A footer stem leaves the root
  along the bottom of the
  page. Leaves are hung at the nodes, every $1.4$ to $2.2\,$cm,
  alternately left and right,
  $35^\circ$ to $60^\circ$ off the stem, $2.4\,$cm long at the bottom
  of the page and $1.3\,$cm at
  the top, and only where there is room.
\end{construction}

\begin{definition}
  A \emph{proxy} for a motif is a disc of radius $r(s)$, where $s$ is
  the motif's scale, that
  approximates the area it occupies. A point $p$ of the free region
  $R$ has \emph{free
  radius}
  \[
    f(p)=\min\Bigl(\,d(p,\partial R),\ \min_i\bigl(\lVert
    p-c_i\rVert-\kappa\,r_i\bigr)\Bigr),
  \]
  the radius of the largest disc about $p$ that stays inside $R$ and
  overlaps no proxy
  $(c_i,r_i)$ by more than the fraction $1-\kappa$. With
  $\kappa=0.85$, a leaf may tuck under
  the edge of a flower; stems are proxies too, as chains of small discs.
\end{definition}

\begin{construction}[Growth]
  The region $R$ is the margin column, a band along the bottom of the
  page, and on the last
  page also the rectangle between the end of the text and the footer.
  On a grid of candidates
  spaced $2\,$mm apart, each point keeps $f(p)$ and its distance to
  the nearest stem. Then,
  until the largest free radius falls below $3.6\,$mm:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item take the point $p$ that maximizes $f(p)$ among those within
      $3\,$cm of a stem, with
      a random factor in $[0.85,1.15]$ so that ties break differently
      on every seed;
    \item choose a motif whose smallest proxy fits, by weight; a
      motif that has already
      appeared $k$ times has its weight divided by $1+k/c$, and only
      flowers, lilies,
      umbels and bells have a hard cap. Set its scale so that
      $r(s)\approx0.95\,f(p)$;
    \item give it an axis pointing away from the nearest stem point,
      leaning upward for the
      kinds that reach for the light, and grow a branch to it from
      that point, as in the
      authored composition. The branch becomes a stem for later branches.
  \end{enumerate}
  Because the largest hole is always taken first, flowers and lilies
  are placed early,
  sprays and leaves fill the middle, and a last pass puts pairs of
  leaflets, buds and small
  clusters of florets on the stems wherever a hole of more than
  $1.7\,$mm is left next to
  one; a hole far from any stem may get one of six copper accents,
  and otherwise stays empty.
  That ordering is the whole reason the result looks \emph{composed}
  rather than scattered.
\end{construction}

\begin{remark}[Adapting to the text]
  The free region is computed from the page layout, so changing the
  margins moves the
  garden. The end of the text is measured where it happens:
  \verb|\florilegiumtextend|
  records \verb|\pagetotal| and \verb|\pagedepth|, the height of the
  material already on the
  current page, and the background of that page is drawn after it. On
  the last page, the
  garden therefore spills into the space below the words in a single
  compilation: branches
  arch out of the main stems into that space and each ends in a flower.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Visitors, marks and seasons]
  Butterflies and bees take holes like any motif but need no branch;
  a snail is put on a
  main stem and, on about one page in five, a bird on a twig of its
  own. Their decisions come
  from a random stream of their own, so a page without them is the
  page it would have been.
  A mark in the text, left by \verb|\florilegiumbloom| or by a
  section heading, is found in
  the page box at shipout and gets its flower before any leaf is
  hung. An age between $0$
  and $1$ reweights the motifs from buds in spring to seed heads in autumn.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}[Cost]
  The layout is cheap: the grid has a few thousand points, a proxy
  only updates the points
  near it, and the whole loop takes a fraction of a second. This
  document of four pages
  compiles in about five seconds.
\end{remark}
